## The Human Authorship Rule and the "Black Box" Problem

The absolute foundation of modern U.S. copyright law is that copyright requires human authorship. This principle was legally cemented when the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed in Thaler v. Perlmutter that copyright cannot protect works created by non-humans (a ruling the Supreme Court declined to review in 2026).

The Copyright Office report explains that copyright is designed to incentivize human creativity, meaning purely AI-generated outputs automatically enter the public domain. This is due to the "black box" problem: when a user inputs a text prompt, the AI’s neural networks autonomously and unpredictably execute the final expressive details (the exact words, colors, or pixels). Because the user lacks direct "expressive control" over this execution, typing a prompt—no matter how complex or iterative—is legally treated as an exercise in conception rather than execution. "Re-rolling" prompts until a desirable output appears is legally viewed as rolling dice, not authoring a work.

## Copyright vs. Contract

A common pitfall for creators is confusing contractual terms with copyright law. While AI platforms (like ChatGPT or Midjourney) often state in their Terms of Service that "you own your outputs," this only governs the private contract between the user and the platform (e.g., granting commercial use rights). It does not, and cannot, grant federal copyright protection. If a third party copies a purely AI-generated asset, the creator cannot sue for copyright infringement because the work lacks human authorship under federal law.

## The Four Ways to Copyright Protection

Creators can still legally protect projects that incorporate generative AI, provided there is substantial human creative contribution. The Copyright Office outlines four clear pathways where AI usage does not strip a work of protection:

1. **Assistive Use:** Utilizing AI for non-expressive technical tasks, such as grammar checking, debugging code, or basic image upscaling.
2. **Expressive Inputs:** Feeding a creator's own pre-existing, copyrighted material (like a personal sketch or draft) into an AI. Protection remains active over any of the original human expression that remains perceptible in the output.
3. **Compilations and Arrangements:** Selecting and arranging various AI elements into a larger, creative layout (like a comic book or graphic novel). While the individual AI elements remain unprotected, the creative arrangement as a whole is copyrightable.
4. **Creative Modifications:** Substantially altering an AI output through human effort, such as manually rewriting AI text or digitally painting over a generated image. Only the human-made modifications are protected.

## Best Practices for Creators

To successfully navigate this landscape, businesses and creators must practice honest disclosure when registering with the Copyright Office, explicitly disclaiming AI-generated components. Additionally, creators should meticulously document their iterative process—keeping drafts, logs, and edit histories—to prove exactly where human modification and creative control took over from the machine.

The U.S. Copyright Office expressly stated the premise, and the legal reality of the "public domain" is the direct, automatic consequence of that premise.

1. What the Copyright Office Expressly Said

In its Part 2 Report, the U.S. Copyright Office explicitly stated:
- "Copyright does not extend to purely AI-generated material, or material where there is insufficient human control over the expressive elements."
- They concluded that a work must be the product of a human mind to be recognized under the Copyright Act. Because the AI is doing the "execution" of the art or text, the output is not legally a "work of authorship."

2. Is it "Not Copyrightable" or "Public Domain"?

In intellectual property law, these two terms are functionally inseparable for a new creation.
When a brand-new creative asset (like an image, poem, or song) is generated, there are only two legal states it can occupy:

- It is protected by copyright (meaning someone owns the exclusive right to copy, distribute, or sell it).
- It is in the public domain (meaning it belongs to the public, no one owns a copyright, and anyone is legally allowed to use, copy, or sell it without permission).

Because the Copyright Office explicitly rules that purely AI-generated content is not copyrightable, it cannot occupy state #1. Therefore, by default, it immediately enters state #2: the public domain.

## How This Works in the Real World

The Copyright Office’s report explicitly warns that because these outputs lack human authorship, they are free for anyone to use.

If you use an AI tool to generate a beautiful digital painting from a prompt, you cannot register it for a copyright. If a competitor takes that exact image, prints it on T-shirts, and sells them, you cannot sue them for copyright infringement. Legally, that image is treated exactly like a play by Shakespeare or a photograph from the 1800s—it belongs to the public domain, and anyone can use it.

## The unresolved edge

The line between "assisted" and "purely AI-generated" is still blurry in practice. Legal analysts at Mayer Brown note that neither the Copyright Office nor the courts have set a bright-line threshold for how much human input is enough, and a pending case, *Allen v. Perlmutter*, tests whether over 600 rounds of prompt refinement can amount to authorship. Until that's resolved, the safest practice for creators is the one the Copyright Office itself recommends: disclose AI involvement honestly when registering a work, and keep a paper trail — drafts, edit histories, logs — showing exactly where your own hand took over from the machine.
