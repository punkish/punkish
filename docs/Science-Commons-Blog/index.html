    Below are most of the blog posts written by me at Science Commons between 2010 and 2015.

    Law and the GeoWeb: A Workshop on IP and Geographic Data in the Internet Era Sponsored by Creative Commons and the United States Geological Survey

    November 19, 2010

    This post recaps a collaborative workshop co-hosted with the USGS in Washington, D.C., addressing the legal and technical frictions around online geographic data. As spatial data shifted to distributed web platforms, proprietary rights, conflicting terms of service, and database laws threatened interoperability. Participants explored how to balance public domain government mapping with crowdsourced spatial data, emphasizing standardized open licensing and CC0 dedications to enable seamless data mashups for environmental monitoring and emergency response.

    Workshop on Open Government, Open Data, Open Source, and Open Standards

    May 5, 2011

    Examining the convergence of open civic technology, this article argues that open government, open data, open-source software, and open standards must function together rather than in administrative silos. Agencies often mandate transparency while locking data behind proprietary formats or restrictive terms. By aligning unencumbered public data releases (using CC0 and CC BY) with open-source tools and transparent technical standards, governments can dismantle bureaucratic bottlenecks, eliminate proprietary vendor lock-in, and empower public accountability.

    Introducing the CC Science Advisory Board

    November 16, 2012

    This announcement introduces the newly formed Creative Commons Science Advisory Board, established to ground CC’s legal tools and policy campaigns directly in the operational realities of active scientific research. Composed of international experts across bioinformatics, genomics, publishing, and data curation, the board guides CC’s strategy on research data sharing, text and data mining, and institutional reform. The post stresses that accelerating discovery requires moving beyond static PDFs toward legally interoperable digital infrastructure.

    New Zealand Open Data Conference

    December 22, 2012

    Reporting from the New Zealand Open Data Conference, this post examines the country’s emergence as a pioneer in public data policy through its New Zealand Government Open Access and Licensing (NZGOAL) framework. NZGOAL established clear, proactive standards for releasing public sector information under CC BY and raw datasets under CC0. The article positions New Zealand’s deliberate, structured policy approach as a scalable global model for turning abstract open-government goals into practical administrative routine.

    PLOS and figshare Make Open Science Publishing More Open

    January 31, 2013

    This post highlights the integration between PLOS and cloud repository figshare to seamlessly host, index, and publish supplemental scientific data alongside research articles. By assigning persistent DOIs and applying standardized open licenses (CC BY and CC0) to supplementary figures, tables, and datasets, the partnership converted static, neglected research files into citable, machine-readable digital assets. It marked a key step toward transforming scholarly communication into an interconnected, computational web of discovery.

    The (Net)Works Effect: Open Data Day 2013

    February 22, 2013

    Reflecting on International Open Data Day 2013, this post explores the compounding network effects created when civic technologists, researchers, and public servants mobilize simultaneously worldwide. Every openly licensed public dataset released increases the value of existing civic data, powering regional dashboards for transit, public health, and municipal budgets. The piece frames Open Data Day not just as a coding event, but as a cultural movement compelling governments to adopt open data as baseline civic infrastructure.

    Why Open Science Training Matters

    March 5, 2013

    This post highlights the persistent educational gap in academic curricula regarding open research practices. Despite rising institutional mandates for data sharing, researchers rarely receive formal training in data curation, version control, or open licensing, leading many to default to closed practices out of legal confusion. The article calls on universities, funders, and scholarly societies to institutionalize open science literacy, arguing that systemic cultural openness cannot succeed without equipping researchers with foundational digital tools.

    Doubling Down on Markdown for Science

    June 10, 2013

    Advocating for lightweight, plain-text authoring in scientific research, this post makes the case for embedding Markdown into academic writing workflows. Legacy word processors and proprietary formats lock up text and break automated pipelines. Combined with Git and Pandoc, Markdown provides a transparent, human-readable format that seamlessly integrates prose, executable code, and datasets. Adopting Markdown streamlines publishing, prevents vendor lock-in, and supports reproducible computational scholarship.

    State of the Map Is Alive and Well

    June 11, 2013

    Reporting from State of the Map, this post celebrates the maturity and resilience of the OpenStreetMap (OSM) community. Crowdsourced spatial mapping evolved from an experimental project into vital global infrastructure supporting civic planning and life-saving disaster relief through the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team (HOT). The recap underscores the community's successful transition to the Open Database License (ODbL) and demonstrates how open collaboration can out-innovate and outlast proprietary alternatives.

    Change Will Come and Manylabs Will Play an Important Part

    August 7, 2013

    Spotlighting open hardware and grassroots science, this post covers Manylabs, an incubator and makerspace focused on democratizing scientific instrumentation. By designing low-cost, open-source environmental sensors and openly licensed educational curricula, Manylabs enabled students, educators, and community scientists to monitor local air, water, and soil conditions directly. The piece highlights how affordable open hardware breaks down financial barriers in research and fosters a socially engaged scientific culture.

    Frank Warmerdam: Leading Open Geospatial Community by Action

    August 7, 2013

    This profile honors software developer Frank Warmerdam, the primary creator of GDAL/OGR (Geospatial Data Abstraction Library). Warmerdam's open-source translation layer solved proprietary format fragmentation, quietly underpinning virtually all modern GIS software, commercial mapping tools, and open spatial science. The post celebrates his ethos of leading through consistent engineering and mentorship within the Open Source Geospatial Foundation (OSGeo), showing how dedicated contributors build foundational digital infrastructure.

    Open Science Course: A Cool, Connected Science Experience

    September 30, 2013

    Reviewing an innovative online open science course, this post highlights an experiential model where students, researchers, and mentors collaborated openly across web platforms. Participants engaged in hands-on work with open repositories, preregistrations, open peer review, and Creative Commons licensing. The article notes that this interactive environment demystified pre-publication sharing and licensing, proving that open science is best taught as an active, collaborative practice rather than an abstract theory.

    Identifying Drug Targets One Protein at a Time

    November 19, 2013

    Focusing on the pre-competitive biomedical research model of the Structural Genomics Consortium (SGC), this post examines how public-private partnerships can accelerate drug discovery. Rather than hoarding early-stage protein structures behind proprietary patents, SGC places chemical probes, structural data, and research tools directly into the public domain. This unencumbered commons prevents redundant effort across competing pharmaceutical firms, lowers research costs, and accelerates the validation of critical therapeutic targets.

    Human Services Taxonomy

    December 2, 2013

    Addressing fragmentation across social welfare systems, this post explores the Open Eligibility project and the need for a shared, openly licensed Human Services Taxonomy. People in need and social caseworkers often face siloed directories when searching for housing, food, and healthcare assistance, often locked down by proprietary vocabularies. By releasing a unified taxonomy under Creative Commons terms, Open Eligibility enabled municipalities and civic developers to build interoperable directories, ensuring vulnerable populations connect with essential support services without friction.

    BioMed Central Moves to CC BY 4.0 Along with CC0 for Data

    December 18, 2013

    This post celebrates BioMed Central upgrading its open-access journal portfolio to Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) and formally adopting CC0 for underlying experimental data. The 4.0 suite introduced explicit international coverage for database rights and streamlined attribution for automated research. Pairing CC BY for narrative text with CC0 for data solved attribution stacking in computational research, establishing an industry benchmark for balancing author credit with seamless data reuse.

    Paleobiology Database Now CC BY

    December 19, 2013

    Highlighting a milestone for earth sciences, this post reports on the Paleobiology Database (PBDB) adopting a standardized CC BY license across its global repository of fossil and geological strata records. Decades of international volunteer curation had previously been encumbered by ambiguous usage terms. Moving to CC BY provided legal certainty, enabling evolutionary biologists and geologists to harvest records computationally, combine them with modern climate and biodiversity models, and set a strong standard for natural history data collections.

    CC Is Now a Group on Earth Observations (GEO) Participating Organization

    January 16, 2014

    Announcing Creative Commons' admission as a Participating Organization in the Group on Earth Observations (GEO), this post explores CC's role in shaping international environmental data policy. Integrating planetary satellite imagery, oceanographic feeds, and climate monitoring requires legal interoperability across international boundaries. By joining GEO, Creative Commons advocated for default open licensing (CC0 and CC BY) across the Global Earth Observation System of Systems (GEOSS), ensuring planetary observations can be freely used for disaster response and climate mitigation.

    Publish for Life for Less than the Cost of Your Cable TV Subscription

    February 12, 2014

    Examining economic innovation in scholarly publishing, this post looks at PeerJ's low-cost lifetime membership model. While legacy open-access publishers routinely charged thousands of dollars in author processing charges (APCs), PeerJ introduced an affordable one-time membership fee paired with rigorous peer review, preprint hosting, and CC BY licensing. The article highlights how modern cloud workflows and lean business models can make open-access publishing financially sustainable while eliminating cost barriers for underfunded global researchers.

    Seeds of Change

    May 21, 2014

    This post examines the intersection of open-source principles and agriculture through the launch of the Open Source Seed Initiative (OSSI). Confronting corporate patenting and genetic enclosure of agricultural seed stocks, OSSI created a viral open pledge printed on seed packets. The pledge guarantees the freedom to grow, breed, and share descendant seeds on the condition that subsequent varieties remain equally unencumbered. The post highlights this initiative as a creative application of commons-based ethics to protect plant biodiversity and public food security.

    Liberating the Haystack for the Needles

    June 2, 2014

    Addressing the untapped potential of automated Text and Data Mining (TDM) across scientific literature, this essay argues that "the right to read is the right to mine." While algorithmic scraping can uncover hidden connections across millions of research papers, legacy publishers routinely block automated crawlers using restrictive contracts and proprietary APIs. The post champions Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) licensing as the vital legal antidote that guarantees computational researchers the freedom to crawl, parse, and analyze scientific knowledge without publisher gatekeeping.

    CC Signs Bouchout Declaration for Open Biodiversity

    July 11, 2014

    Covering Creative Commons' signing of the Bouchout Declaration for Open Biodiversity Knowledge Management, this post explores the global campaign to digitize and link biodiversity assets. Natural history institutions house billions of physical specimens, but their data historically remained siloed under fragmented terms. The declaration committed institutions to persistent digital identifiers and standardized open licenses—specifically CC0 for factual observations and CC BY for taxonomic literature—to empower global research tracking species loss, ecological shifts, and environmental preservation.

    Examining Deficiencies of and Limitations on Data Sharing

    August 18, 2014

    Critiquing systemic gaps in academic data-sharing mandates, this post examines why data availability policies frequently lead to superficial compliance. Researchers often rely on vague "upon reasonable request" clauses or deposit undocumented files in proprietary formats, disincentivized by an academic tenure system that rewards journal publications over data stewardship. The piece argues that building a functional data commons requires reforming academic career incentives, actively auditing compliance, and adopting clear public domain tools like CC0 to guarantee genuine computational reuse.

    Vía con Datos

    October 5, 2014

    Reporting on the Vía con Datos civic hackathon in Bogotá, Colombia, this post explores how open public transit data improves urban mobility in congested metropolitan centers. By releasing municipal transit routes, schedules, and GPS feeds under Creative Commons licenses, city officials enabled civic technologists and transit riders to build tailored navigation and transit timing apps. The post highlights this event as proof that open data serves as practical, cost-effective infrastructure that enhances public services and civic engagement across the Global South.

    CC Science’s Indian November

    October 28, 2014

    Previewing an outreach trip across India, this post outlines Creative Commons Science’s engagement with research institutions, academic libraries, and government agencies in New Delhi, Bangalore, and Chennai. The tour aimed to promote institutional open-access mandates, educate scholars on copyright retention, and implement standard public domain data releases using CC0. The article highlights India’s immense potential to bypass legacy proprietary publishing models, using open-source tools and open licensing to foster equitable scientific collaboration across both local and global research communities.

    The Voyager Golden Record

    November 8, 2014

    Reflecting on the Voyager Golden Record launched into deep space in 1977, this essay exposes the terrestrial absurdities of copyright law. While Carl Sagan's team successfully sent this cultural snapshot of humanity to extraterrestrial civilizations, commercial copyright restrictions prevented people on Earth from legally buying or accessing the complete compilation for decades. The post highlights the contrast between humanity’s cosmic ambition to share its collective culture and the restrictive intellectual property frameworks that enclose it at home, emphasizing the need for an enduring public commons.

    SciDataCon 2014 Recap

    November 16, 2014

    Recapping SciDataCon 2014 in New Delhi, this post reviews global discussions on scientific data infrastructure, research stewardship, and sustainability research co-organized by CODATA and the World Data System. The article highlights Creative Commons' advocacy for open licensing standards, particularly CC0 for public datasets, to eliminate friction across interdisciplinary computational research. Addressing the digital divide between the Global North and South, the recap emphasizes that building a modern data-driven research ecosystem requires shared data standards, professional recognition for curation, and sustained multilateral investment.
