I served in an official capacity with Creative Commons for six years—initially as a Science Commons Fellow and subsequently leading Science Commons. Retrospectively, it is evident how the discourse on open science matured during this period. What originated as an initiative to promote zero- or some-rights-reserved copyright licenses evolved into a global awareness of participatory science. Openness transitioned from applying permissive licenses to scientific outputs toward enabling others to build upon them. Although intangible and difficult to quantify, this shift was, in my assessment, more consequential.  
  
To be clear, we did achieve some measurable milestones. The number Creative Commons licenses in use, while challenging to determine precisely, represented the most visible indicator. Several high-profile developments were particularly significant: the Paleobiology Database’s [adoption of CC BY](pdb) transformed an extensive fossil record with an API into a corpus facilitating new discoveries through reuse. BioMed Central’s [transition to CC BY for content and CC0 for data](bmc) established a standard for reconciling author attribution with seamless data reuse. Although OpenStreetMap adopted a non-CC open license, it demonstrated [the potential of more than a million contributors](osm) building a freely accessible global map. Through workshops in [Nottingham](ntg) and [Seattle](sea) and conferences such as [Condatos in Mexico City](mex) and [SciDataCon in New Delhi](del), we convened researchers, civic technologists, and policymakers, confirming that open science held broad relevance beyond a limited circle of converts.  
  
Retrospectively, the intangible elements of this initiative are more discernible than the technical ones. In my assessment, the scientists had little interest in legal nuances. For them, getting attribution for their work was key. For better or for worse, attribution remains the principal currency of scientific careers, underpinning promotions and funding. The Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) license appealed to researchers by ensuring recognition, whereas CC0 proved a harder sell despite its legal appropriateness for scientific data.    
  
Creative Commons represented values that resonated broadly. It functioned as an aspirational community associated with camaraderie, generosity, collaboration, and the public good. We already wore CC t-shirts (they were always so pretty), made posters, watched films made with CC-footage and listened to CC-licensed music. To this, we added [satellite imagery](sat), [data on the side effects of drugs](sid), [taxonomic treatments](tbk) and [geographic base layers](nat) from global to local. We held hackathons, shared expertise, and [tried new projects](mds). This intangible goodwill contributed as much to open science as any legal framework, shifting openness from a procedural requirement to a shared identity.    

Science Commons is no more, but openness in science remains an ongoing endeavor. Publisher agreements continue to restrict open permissions; text and data mining encounter legal uncertainty; and integrating datasets with divergent terms often results in unintended limitations. Significant concerns regarding privacy, responsible data stewardship, and unequal global access to computational resources underscore the limitations of open licenses in addressing systemic inequities. Furthermore, AI consumes scientific outputs voraciously and, as a modern ouroboros, threatens its own foundation. Many colleagues now worry about this existential challenge as much as they pursue knowledge production.    
  
Nevertheless, openness in science is an irreversible development. Although we have not reached a definitive destination, and we never might, the momentum we generated will persist. We helped establish cultural foundations demonstrating that science can be participatory and universally accessible. Legal obstacles endure but the impulse to share and the desire for collective belonging are deeply embedded in our cultural consciousness. This constitutes a meaningful legacy for the future.
